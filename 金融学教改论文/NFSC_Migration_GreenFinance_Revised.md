# Teaching Reform of Finance Course toward the Dual-Carbon Goals: A Migration Study of the NFSC Four-Dimensional Framework in Green Output Chain

1. Chengdu Ginkgo Hotel Management College, Chengdu 611743, China

**Abstract**

China's carbon trading market surpassed RMB 240 billion in cumulative transactions by late 2023, while green credit balances exceeded RMB 30 trillion, signaling a structural shift in the knowledge base required of financial professionals. This paper reports a cross-disciplinary migration of the Narrative-Framework-Scenario-Creation (NFSC) four-dimensional teaching method from engineering education to an undergraduate finance course. We construct a "Green Output Chain"—a progressive cognitive pathway mapping NFSC dimensions to green finance content—and implement a 12-hour module for 48 accounting majors. Student accuracy in applying the five-dimension diagnostic framework rose from 54.5% (24/44) to 90.5% (38/42) (McNemar test, p < 0.001, Cohen's g = 0.18). Scenario-based learning received the highest impact rating (4.5/5.0; chi-square = 9.33, p = 0.025). Drawing on Perkins and Salomon's transfer theory, we propose a four-element migration mechanism—narrative replacement, framework remapping, scenario type conversion, and output adaptation—that preserves NFSC's structural integrity while enabling domain-specific re-localization.

**Keywords**: NFSC teaching method; green output chain; finance education reform; cross-disciplinary migration; dual-carbon goals

## 1 Introduction

By the end of 2023, China's national carbon emission trading market had accumulated over RMB 240 billion in transactions [1]. Green credit balances surpassed RMB 30 trillion [2]. ESG investing has moved from a niche concern to a mainstream criterion in institutional portfolios. These figures signal a structural shift in the knowledge base required of financial professionals: understanding how carbon allowance prices affect loan covenants, how green bond premiums influence corporate financing costs, and how ESG ratings interact with credit spreads is no longer optional.

The gap between this demand and current undergraduate finance instruction is measurable. The dominant textbook structure—money, credit, interest rates, financial markets, instruments, and regulation—relegates green finance to a terminal chapter frequently skipped under time constraints [3]. Students who can accurately recite the Capital Asset Pricing Model cannot explain why a carbon allowance price decline of RMB 10 per ton might trigger early loan recall at a biomass power plant. Students who can compute discounted cash flows cannot articulate why CATL's 2021 green bond carried a coupon rate approximately 50 basis points below its comparable conventional debt.

This pedagogical gap is compounded by a methodological one. A 2022 global survey by the Principles for Responsible Management Education found that 68% of business school signatories to the Sustainable Finance Education Charter had not yet integrated scenario-based learning into their sustainable finance curricula [15]. The Network for Greening the Financial System identified pedagogical innovation in climate finance education as a priority gap in its 2023 report [16]. Within China specifically, green finance topics, when covered in undergraduate finance courses, are almost exclusively taught through lecture, with no documented use of scenario-based or experiential pedagogies [3].

At the same time, a promising pedagogical innovation has emerged from an unexpected source. Huang, Xie, and Sun [4] proposed the Narrative-Framework-Scenario-Creation (NFSC) four-dimensional teaching method in an engineering education context. Using documentary novel excerpts as narrative triggers (Narrative), mismatch-repair diagnostic matrices as analytical tools (Framework), role-playing simulations for skill application (Scenario), and a three-track output system for knowledge transformation (Creation), the method achieved positive outcomes in a graduate-level environmental engineering course. In their discussion, the authors posed an open question: can NFSC migrate to disciplines beyond engineering? They identified three migration preconditions—a narratable material repository, an abstractable knowledge framework, and instructor capacity for scenario design—but provided no actual cross-disciplinary validation.

Our study addresses this gap by answering the open question. We migrated NFSC from its originating engineering context to an undergraduate finance course through the construction of a "Green Output Chain"—a structured mapping of NFSC dimensions to green finance learning outputs. We implemented a 12-hour green finance module with 48 accounting majors and collected pre-post framework usage data, output distribution data, and perceptual feedback. Based on the migration data, we propose a four-element migration mechanism model grounded in Perkins and Salomon's [14] transfer theory.

The research questions guiding this study are:
- RQ1: Can the NFSC four-dimensional teaching method be effectively migrated from environmental engineering to an undergraduate finance course?
- RQ2: What mechanisms govern the cross-disciplinary migration of the NFSC framework?
- RQ3: What is the perceived impact of NFSC dimensions on student learning in the finance course context?

## 2 Literature Review

### 2.1 The NFSC Four-Dimensional Teaching Method

NFSC partitions cognitive progression into four dimensions [4]. Narrative triggers motivation through authentic storytelling; in the prototype course, this meant documentary novel excerpts depicting landfill disputes, incinerator controversies, and community protests [5]. Framework equips learners with structured diagnostic tools—a six-dimension mismatch-repair matrix. Scenario forges application capability through role-playing—EIA hearings, moot courts, and public hearings. Creation transforms knowledge through three output tracks: academic paper, course design project, and pedagogical essay. The four dimensions correspond to ascending levels of Bloom's cognitive taxonomy [6].

Importantly for our migration study, [4] explicitly discussed the generalizability question. The authors argued that NFSC migration to other courses requires three conditions—narratable materials, an abstractable knowledge framework, and instructor scenario design capacity—but acknowledged that "the generalizability of the four-dimensional teaching method awaits validation across more courses, more institutions, and longer teaching cycles." Our study is the first direct response to this call for cross-disciplinary validation.

### 2.2 Green Finance Education: Current State and Challenges

Research on green finance pedagogy remains nascent. The Sustainable Finance Education Charter, signed by 45 business schools globally, reported in 2022 that 68% of signatory institutions had not yet integrated scenario-based learning into their sustainable finance curricula [15]. The NGFS identified pedagogical innovation in climate finance education as a priority research gap [16].

International evidence mirrors these findings. The Sustainable Finance Education Charter, signed by 45 business schools globally, reported in 2022 that 68% of signatory institutions had not yet integrated scenario-based learning into their sustainable finance curricula [15]. The Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS) identified pedagogical innovation in climate finance education as a priority gap [14].

### 2.3 Cross-Disciplinary Teaching Method Transfer

The transfer of teaching methods across disciplinary boundaries has received limited empirical attention in the higher education literature. Perkins and Salomon [14] distinguished between "low-road transfer"—the automatic triggering of well-practiced routines in highly similar contexts—and "high-road transfer"—the mindful abstraction of principles from one context and their deliberate application to another. Cross-disciplinary pedagogical transfer, by this framework, is inherently high-road: it requires instructors to decontextualize a teaching method, identify its invariant structural features, and reconstruct it within the target discipline's specific constraints.

Bransford and Schwartz [15] challenged the traditional "sequestered problem-solving" view of transfer, arguing instead for a "preparation for future learning" perspective that emphasizes how prior learning experiences shape subsequent learning capacity. Under this view, the value of a migrated teaching method lies in both its immediate effectiveness and its capacity to reshape how learners approach problems in the target discipline.

Within engineering education specifically, Crawley et al. [8] documented the international diffusion of the CDIO (Conceive-Design-Implement-Operate) framework across 12 disciplines, noting that successful migrations required "significant recontextualization at the course level" rather than mere adoption. However, CDIO's migration is within the same disciplinary family (engineering); NFSC's migration from engineering to finance represents a more radical cross-disciplinary leap that tests the outer boundary of pedagogical transfer.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Research Design

This study employed a single-group pre-post quasi-experimental design within an authentic classroom setting. The independent variable was the NFSC-based green finance module; the primary dependent variable was student accuracy in applying the five-dimension diagnostic framework. Secondary dependent variables included output track distribution and self-reported learning experience ratings. The study was conducted in the 2024 fall semester at Chengdu Ginkgo Hotel Management College, a private undergraduate institution in Chengdu, China.

### 3.2 Course Context and Participants

*Finance* is a core foundation course for accounting majors (48 contact hours, Huang & Zhang [3] textbook). The study participants were 48 undergraduate accounting students (2023 cohort, Class 6), all nationally admitted. Prior coursework included Fundamental Accounting and Microeconomics. A pre-module diagnostic survey confirmed that student familiarity with green finance was limited to name recognition. Participation in the module was mandatory; survey participation was voluntary and anonymous. Of the 48 enrolled students, 42 completed all four module stages; six were absent due to illness or scheduling conflicts.

### 3.3 Module Design and NFSC Migration

The green finance module was embedded in weeks 10 through 13 of the 16-week semester, occupying 12 contact hours (25% of total course hours). Allocation followed the NFSC structure: Narrative 2h, Framework 4h, Scenario 4h, Creation 2h.

The development of the five-dimension green finance diagnostic framework (Table 2, embedded in the implementation section below) proceeded through three stages. First, we conducted a systematic review of the green finance policy literature [1,2,15,17,18] to identify recurring dimensions relevant to financial decision-making. Second, we adapted standard financial analytical tools (*DuPont analysis*, *Discounted Cash Flow*, *Duration analysis*) to green-finance-specific questions. Third, we validated the framework through consultation with two finance faculty members not involved in the module delivery, who independently reviewed the five dimensions for completeness and conceptual clarity. Both reviewers confirmed that the five dimensions captured the core analytical dimensions of green finance decision-making for undergraduate-level instruction.

### 3.4 Assessment Rubric and Scoring

The primary outcome measure—framework usage accuracy—was assessed using a 5-point rubric (Table 1). Each student output was independently scored by the course instructor and a teaching assistant. Inter-rater agreement was high (Cohen's kappa = 0.84, 95% CI [0.76, 0.92]). For the pre-post comparison, accuracy was defined as correctly applying three or more of the five diagnostic dimensions to a given case with explicit labeling of which dimensions were used and valid analytical reasoning for each.

**Table 1. Framework Usage Accuracy Scoring Rubric**

| Score | Criterion |
|:-----:|-----------|
| 0 | No framework dimensions applied; purely descriptive or opinion-based analysis |
| 1 | One dimension applied with valid reasoning |
| 2 | Two dimensions applied with valid reasoning |
| 3 | Three dimensions applied with valid reasoning for each; dimensions explicitly labeled |
| 4 | Four dimensions applied with valid reasoning for each; cross-dimensional interaction noted |
| 5 | Five dimensions applied with valid reasoning for each; at least two cross-dimensional interactions analyzed |

For the "most impactful dimension" survey question, students selected exactly one of four options (Narrative, Framework, Scenario, or Creation), yielding a multinomial distribution that we tested against a uniform null hypothesis (expected proportion = 25% per option).

### 3.5 Data Collection Instruments

Three data collection instruments were employed:

- **Pre-post diagnostic test** (Weeks 11 and 12): Students analyzed a standardized green finance case study (a photovoltaic equipment manufacturer seeking a RMB 50 million green technology upgrade loan). The same case was used for both pre- and post-test to control for case-difficulty variance, with a three-week interval between administrations to minimize memory effects.

- **Post-module survey** (Week 16): An anonymous questionnaire was distributed online. Items included a 5-point Likert scale for each NFSC dimension's perceived learning gain, a forced-choice "most impactful dimension" item, and two open-ended reflection questions. Response rate was 75.0% (36/48).

- **Student output tracking**: All three-track outputs were collected, scored using the rubric, and analyzed for framework usage patterns and track distribution.

### 3.6 The Green Output Chain

We define the Green Output Chain (GOC) as the progressive, assessable sequence of learning outputs generated by mapping each NFSC dimension onto green finance content. The chain comprises four stages: (1) Green cognitive engagement, where narrative texts on environmental-financial events trigger emotional resonance and problem awareness; (2) Green diagnostic competence, where the five-dimension framework enables structured analysis of green finance cases; (3) Green decision experience, where simulated credit approval and carbon trading scenarios produce behavioral learning; and (4) Green solution creation, where student-generated financing plans and research reports demonstrate applied competence. Each stage maps to a specific NFSC dimension and produces a distinct, evaluable output. The construct validity of the GOC rests on demonstrated structural parallelism between the engineering prototype [4] and the finance migration (Table 2), verified through the expert review process described in §3.3. The GOC differs from generic learning outcome taxonomies (e.g., Bloom's [6]) in that each stage is operationalized through a specific pedagogical technique—narrative reading, framework exercises, role-play simulation, and open-ended project work—rather than through abstract cognitive verbs alone.

## 4 Implementation and Results

### 4.1 Module Delivery

**Narrative (Week 10).** Three narrative texts were distributed before class: (a) a Shandong biomass power plant that faced early loan recall when carbon allowance prices dropped below operational breakeven, shrinking annual carbon revenue by approximately RMB 4 million (adapted from a 36Kr industry report); (b) CATL's RMB 5 billion green medium-term note offering circular (abridged); (c) a Jiangsu wastewater treatment plant that lost approximately RMB 3 million in carbon trading revenue due to undervalued emission allowances. Each text included three guided questions. The first 20 minutes of class were devoted to extracting academic problems from visceral narratives.

**Framework (Weeks 11-12).** The instructor introduced the five-dimension framework (Table 2).

**Table 2. Five-Dimension Green Finance Diagnostic Framework**

| Dimension | Core Question | Key Indicators | Analytical Tools | Case Illustration |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|------------------|-------------------|
| Financing Accessibility | Can a green project obtain adequate and reasonably priced capital? | Debt ratio < 70%; credit line/investment > 1.2; green vs. conventional loan spread | *DuPont analysis*, *DCF* | CATL 2021 green bond: AAA-rated, 3.28% coupon, ~50bp below conventional |
| Carbon Asset Valuation | Are emission allowances and related green assets priced correctly? | Allowance price/marginal abatement cost ratio; carbon assets/total assets | *DCF valuation*, *Real options* | China national CEA: 55-65 CNY/ton, intra-year volatility ~18% |
| Policy Incentive Transmission | Do green policy preferences effectively reach project financials? | Tax relief/revenue; interest subsidy/financial expense | *Sensitivity analysis*, *Scenario simulation* | Green loan subsidies: ~RMB 120B cumulative (2018-2023) [2] |
| Environmental Disclosure | Does environmental data transparency affect financing costs? | ESG rating tier; disclosure completeness index | *Regression analysis*, *Event study* | One-notch ESG upgrade ~30bp reduction in financing cost [7] |
| Maturity-Liquidity Matching | Does the long-term nature of green projects align with financial instruments? | Current ratio > 1.5; payback period/loan term; debt maturity split | *Cash flow matching*, *Duration analysis* | Solar farm: 8-12 yr payback vs. 5-10 yr max green loan tenor |

The baseline pre-test on Week 11 yielded an accuracy rate of 54.5% (24/44 students correctly applied three or more dimensions). After two additional guided practice sessions using three different green finance cases, the Week 12 post-test showed an accuracy rate of 90.5% (38/42 students). A McNemar test on the paired data (n = 40 students with both pre- and post-test scores) was statistically significant (p < 0.001, Cohen's g = 0.18, indicating a medium-to-large effect).

**Scenario (Week 13).** The class was divided into four role groups: corporate CFO team (9 students), bank credit review team (9 students), third-party environmental auditor team (9 students), and observer team (15 students). Each team received 20 minutes for preparation and 30 minutes for an open inquiry session. The instructor did not prescribe an outcome; the loan approval decision depended on argument quality.

Observer team records showed that the corporate CFO team referenced the framework an average of 4.2 times during the session, and the bank credit review team an average of 5.3 times, indicating the framework had become shared analytical vocabulary between opposing roles.

**Creation (Weeks 14-16).** Students produced outputs through three tracks: Track A (green bond or ESG fund research report), Track B (green financing plan for a listed company), and Track C (teaching reflection). All tracks required explicit application of three or more diagnostic dimensions with dimension labeling.

### 4.2 Student Outputs

The three-track distribution was: Track A 26.2% (11/42), Track B 61.9% (26/42), Track C 11.9% (5/42). This contrasts notably with the distribution reported in the engineering prototype [4]—where the corresponding tracks were 40%, 35%, and 25% respectively—suggesting that output track preferences reflect discipline-specific cognitive styles. Accounting students' strong preference for Track B (structured financial analysis) aligns with their disciplinary disposition toward analytical report writing.

Framework usage in Track B outputs showed that the carbon asset valuation dimension was the most frequently applied (92.3%, 24/26), followed by policy incentive transmission (80.8%, 21/26), financing accessibility (73.1%, 19/26), maturity-liquidity matching (61.5%, 16/26), and environmental disclosure (50.0%, 13/26). Eight students (19.0%, 8/42) spontaneously proposed analytical dimensions beyond the prescribed five—international carbon tariff risk (n = 4) and technology iteration risk (n = 3). In the NFSC framework, spontaneous framework extension is identified as an indicator of the Creation dimension [4].

The mean rubric score across all Track B outputs was 3.8 (SD = 0.9), indicating that the typical student correctly applied three to four dimensions with valid reasoning. The maximum score of 5 was achieved by 4 students (9.5%).

### 4.3 Student Perceptions

The post-module survey (n = 36, 75.0% response rate) yielded the results shown in Table 3.

**Table 3. Student Perceived Learning Gains Across NFSC Dimensions (n = 36)**

| NFSC Dimension | Mean Rating (1-5) | "Most Impactful" Votes | Representative Student Comment |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|------|
| Narrative | 4.2 | 10 (27.8%) | "The biomass plant story made carbon prices tangible—they can shut down a factory." |
| Framework | 3.9 | 6 (16.7%) | "The framework provides structure, but I needed more practice to internalize all five dimensions." |
| Scenario | 4.5 | 15 (41.7%) | "When the CFO argued their case, I had to counter using specific framework dimensions. That pressure made the framework real." |
| Creation | 4.0 | 5 (13.9%) | "Choosing my own company for the financing plan made me actually care about the analysis." |

A one-sample chi-square goodness-of-fit test rejected the null hypothesis that students chose among the four dimensions uniformly, chi-square (3, n = 36) = 9.33, p = 0.025. The Scenario dimension received significantly more "most impactful" votes (41.7%) than expected under random selection (25.0%).

These results align with the finding in [4] that students consistently rank scenario immersion as the most memorable learning experience. A Track B student noted: "Before, loan figures on financial statements were just numbers. Now I know behind them are carbon quota prices, policy subsidies, ESG ratings—they are not dead digits."

## 5 Discussion

### 5.1 NFSC Cross-Disciplinary Migration: A Four-Element Mechanism

The migration data support a four-element mechanism for NFSC cross-disciplinary transfer. Perkins and Salomon's [14] distinction between low-road and high-road transfer provides the theoretical lens: pedagogical migration across disciplines that differ in their core knowledge structures (engineering vs. finance) requires high-road transfer—the deliberate abstraction of structural principles and their re-application in a new context.

The four elements we identified are:

1. **Narrative replacement**. The source material type shifts from engineering conflict narratives to the target discipline's narrative events. Adherence to Perkins and Salomon's high-road transfer requires that the replacement materials contain authentic economic or social conflicts with sufficient emotional tension to trigger the cognitive engagement that NFSC's Narrative dimension demands.

2. **Framework remapping**. Diagnostic dimensions are reconstructed from the original discipline's knowledge categories to the target discipline's analytical tools. This element aligns with Bransford and Schwartz's [17] "preparation for future learning" concept: the remapped framework should equip learners not just for the specific module tasks but for approaching unfamiliar green finance problems beyond the course.

3. **Scenario type conversion**. The simulation type converts from engineering decision-making to the target discipline's prototypical professional decisions. The conversion must preserve the key NFSC criterion—multi-party interest conflict and information incompleteness—while adapting the surface features to the target profession's practice.

4. **Output adaptation**. Output types adjust to match discipline-specific learning styles. In our case, accounting students' preference for structured financial plan writing (61.9% Track B) reflects their disciplinary cognitive disposition, validating the adaptation principle.

The four elements are sequential but interdependent. Framework remapping depends on narrative replacement because the diagnostic dimensions must be operable on the narrative materials. Scenario type conversion depends on framework remapping because the scenario must create space for framework application. Output adaptation depends on scenario type conversion because the output must demonstrate synthesis of framework analysis and scenario experience.

### 5.2 Dimension-Specific Adaptation

The four NFSC dimensions showed differential adaptation fit, as evidenced by both the student survey data (Table 3) and implementation observations.

**Narrative** showed high fit (mean rating 4.2/5.0, 27.8% most impactful). Financial history inherently provides rich narrative material—unlike engineering case studies that often require careful reconstruction from technical reports, major financial events (2008 subprime crisis, 2020 negative rates, 2023 SVB collapse) arrive pre-narrativized through extensive media coverage. This reduces the instructor's narrative construction burden.

**Framework** scored lowest among the four dimensions (3.9/5.0, 16.7% most impactful) yet produced the largest measurable learning gain (54.5% to 90.5% accuracy improvement). This apparent paradox—lower perceived value despite higher objective effect—may reflect what the Framework dimension's cognitive demand: students experienced it as effortful but effective.

**Scenario** received the highest ratings (4.5/5.0, 41.7% most impactful, significantly above uniform expectation), consistent with experiential learning theory [12]. The green credit approval simulation's multi-role design created what Lave and Wenger [11] term "legitimate peripheral participation"—students engaged in authentic professional practice under scaffolded conditions.

**Creation** received moderate ratings (4.0/5.0, 13.9% most impactful). The relatively lower "most impactful" share is consistent with [4]'s finding and may reflect the timing of survey administration (immediately after module completion, before students had time to reflect on their output quality).

### 5.3 Implications for Engineering Education

The cross-disciplinary migration offers a bidirectional contribution. **Forward direction**: the successful NFSC migration from engineering to finance provides the first empirical evidence that NFSC's core mechanisms—four-dimension progression, event-bus communication, and three-track output—are not bound to engineering content. This answers the open question posed in [4] and extends NFSC's generalizability claim from "logical possibility" to "empirical demonstration."

**Reverse direction**: two instructional design features from the finance migration may productively feed back into engineering education. First, the structured use of public-domain financial data in our Framework stage (corporate bond prospectuses, carbon market price histories) offers a model for incorporating real-time data into engineering classroom analysis—a practice underutilized in traditional engineering case teaching. Second, the observer-team design in our Scenario stage (a dedicated team that records and analyzes framework usage during the simulation) provides a replicable mechanism for metacognitive scaffolding—learners observing their peers' decision processes while explicitly tracking analytical framework usage.

A concrete example of reverse transfer: an "incinerator plant BOT project financing" scenario could be embedded in environmental engineering courses using the observer-team design. Students would make technical design decisions and simultaneously track how their financial assumptions—discount rate, loan terms, carbon revenue projections—constrain or enable their technical choices,—directly addressing CDIO Standard 4 (Introduction to Engineering) and supporting CEEAA accreditation criteria on "Engineering and Society" and "Environment and Sustainable Development" [19].

## 6 Conclusion

This study provides the first cross-disciplinary empirical validation of the NFSC teaching method, demonstrating its viability beyond its originating engineering discipline. Three principal findings emerge.

First, NFSC migration to an undergraduate finance course is effective. The five-dimension diagnostic framework produced a large pre-post improvement in student analytical accuracy (from 54.5% to 90.5%, p < 0.001, Cohen's g = 0.18), and the Scenario dimension was rated as the most impactful learning experience by a statistically significant margin.

Second, the Green Output Chain concept provides a structured mechanism for coupling NFSC with non-engineering disciplines. The chain's construct validity rests on demonstrated parallelism between the engineering prototype and the finance migration, verified through expert review.

Third, cross-disciplinary NFSC migration follows a four-element mechanism—narrative replacement, framework remapping, scenario type conversion, and output adaptation—grounded in Perkins and Salomon's high-road transfer theory. This mechanism preserves NFSC's structural integrity while enabling domain-specific content re-localization and provides an actionable methodological guide for future NFSC migration to other disciplines.

## 7 Limitations

This study has five primary limitations. (1) **Internal validity**: the single-group pre-post design cannot rule out maturation effects, history effects, or testing effects. The observed improvement from 54.5% to 90.5% may partly reflect increased familiarity with the case format rather than genuine framework acquisition. (2) **External validity**: single-section, single-semester, single-institution design limits generalizability. The 48 students at a private undergraduate college in Chengdu are not representative of finance students nationwide. (3) **Measurement validity**: the framework usage accuracy rubric, while achieving acceptable inter-rater reliability (kappa = 0.84), has not been validated against external criteria such as performance in subsequent finance coursework or professional examinations. (4) **Survey response bias**: the 75.0% response rate raises the possibility that non-respondents (n = 12) differed systematically from respondents. (5) **Module duration**: 12 contact hours provides limited exposure to NFSC's full four-dimension progression; the Framework dimension in particular would benefit from additional practice sessions. (6) **Creation assessment**: the rubric (Table 1) measures framework usage accuracy—primarily a Framework-dimension outcome. The originality, creativity, and professional quality of student outputs in the Creation dimension were not independently scored, which limits the paper's ability to differentiate Framework effects from genuine Creation effects.

Future research should pursue (a) a randomized controlled comparison between NFSC-based and conventional delivery of the same green finance content, (b) multi-iteration longitudinal tracking to assess knowledge retention and transfer beyond the semester, and (c) horizontal expansion to other business disciplines to test the generality of the four-element mechanism.
